## Key claims the report will draw on

Locations are printed JACM pages (add nothing — they are the pages in the 596–615 range).

- **The single-source shortest-path bound is O(n log n + m), with non-negative edge lengths.** Stated in the abstract as result (1), and derived in Section 4. Note the paper's own ordering: `O(n log n + m)`. The report's `O(m + n log n)` is the same bound. (p. 596, Abstract, item (1); p. 610, Sec. 4.)
- **What that bound improved on.** The previous best was Johnson's `O(m log_(m/n+2) n)`, "based on the use of implicit heaps with a branching factor of m/n + 2" — i.e. a *d*-ary heap. So even the bound Fibonacci heaps displaced was itself a data-structure result. (p. 596, Abstract; p. 610, Sec. 4.)
- **The amortized bounds.** delete min and delete are O(log n) amortized; **all other operations, decrease key in particular, are O(1) amortized**. Stated in the abstract, restated in the introduction, and proved as Theorem 1. (p. 596, Abstract; p. 597, Sec. 1; p. 604, Theorem 1.)
- **The heap operation count for Dijkstra is the whole of the analysis.** Section 4 counts, exactly: one make heap, *n* insert, *n* delete min, and **at most *m* decrease key** operations, with maximum heap size n − 1. Then: with an F-heap, heap operations total O(n log n + m), and "the time for other tasks is O(n + m)". This is the report's key structural fact — the non-heap work is linear and fixed, so the *only* term that changes with the container is the heap term. (p. 610, Sec. 4.)
- **Why the asymmetry between decrease key and delete min matters for shortest paths.** The paper's stated motivation is that "in many such applications, the number of deletions is relatively small" relative to total operations, which is exactly the shortest-path profile: *n* deletions against up to *m* decrease keys. This is the argument for why the priority queue governs the bound. (p. 597, Sec. 1.)
- **The authors' framing: this is an implementation, not a new algorithm.** "Our original purpose in developing F-heaps was to speed up Dijkstra's algorithm", and Section 4 is titled as a use of F-heaps "to implement Dijkstra's shortest path algorithm". Section 4 reproduces Dijkstra's Scan step unchanged and swaps only the container. (pp. 597–598, Sec. 1; p. 609, Sec. 4.)
- **decrease key requires knowing the item's position in the heap.** The operation definition states this assumption explicitly, and a Remark on the same page spells out that an auxiliary data structure (a pointer from each item to its heap position, or disjoint-set union if melding is allowed) must supply it. This is the hidden cost that lazy deletion trades away. (p. 597, Sec. 1, operation definitions and Remark.)
- **The improvements are density-dependent.** "All our bounds for network optimization are asymptotic improvements for graphs of intermediate density (n < m << n²)", and the abstract says the results "give asymptotic improvements for graphs of appropriate densities". The advantage of the better priority queue is explicitly a function of m relative to n. (p. 596, Abstract; p. 598, Sec. 1.)
- **Ω(n log n + m) is a lower bound for Dijkstra's algorithm in the comparison model.** "Dijkstra's algorithm requires Ω(n log n + m) time assuming a comparison-based computation model, since it must examine all the edges in the worst case and can be used to sort n numbers." So F-heaps make *the algorithm* optimal, though the authors note this "does not preclude the existence of another, faster algorithm" for the problem. (p. 613, Sec. 6, open question (ii).)
- **The paper does contain a "lazy deletion" mechanism — but it is not the report's.** Section 3's first F-heap variant, "F-heaps with vacant nodes", credited to Cheriton and Tarjan, performs delete min or delete by leaving the node in place and marking it vacant, making deletion O(1); find min then sweeps and destroys the vacant nodes it reaches (Lemma 2 bounds the amortized cost at O(l(log(n/l) + 1)) for *l* nodes destroyed). Its stated drawback is extra space when items are repeatedly inserted and deleted. See Cautions. (pp. 605–606, Sec. 3.)
- **F-heaps extend Vuillemin's binomial queues,** which support all heap operations in O(log n) *worst-case* time (Brown studied representations and both theoretical and experimental running times). Useful if the report wants a sourced sentence on the worst-case/amortized distinction. (p. 597, Sec. 1.)
- **Storage cost is explicit: four pointers per node** in the paper's implementation, reducible to three or, more elaborately, two, "at a cost of a constant factor in running time". Direct evidence that the asymptotically best structure carries real per-node overhead. (p. 604, end of Sec. 2.)

## Relevance to this project

- **This is the source that makes "the data structure IS the algorithm" a citable claim rather than a slogan.** Section 4 (p. 610) does the entire analysis by *counting heap operations*: 1 make heap + *n* insert + *n* delete min + ≤ *m* decrease key, and then "the time for other tasks is O(n + m)". The algorithm's own work is linear and constant across all arms; only the heap term moves. The report's Experiment 1 is exactly this decomposition run empirically, with the container as the independent variable.
- **It supplies the theoretical spine of the size and density sweeps.** The three canonical bounds — O(V²) linear scan, O((V+E) log V) binary heap, O(E + V log V) Fibonacci heap — differ only in the priority queue, and Fredman & Tarjan supply the third and the framework. The paper's own statement that its improvements hold "for graphs of intermediate density (n < m << n²)" (p. 598) is a sourced prediction that the payoff depends on density, which is precisely what the density sweep is built to locate. The crossover the report measures is the empirical shadow of that inequality.
- **It names the operation the lazy-deletion binary heap gives up.** The report's heap pushes a fresh (node, dist) entry on every improvement instead of performing decrease-key. Fredman & Tarjan define decrease key on p. 597 and note it "assumes that the position of i in h is known", requiring an auxiliary index. That is the cost lazy deletion avoids, and it is why the report's complexity claim rests on the lazy variant: without decrease-key the heap can hold up to O(E) entries, so pops are O(log E) = O(log V) for simple graphs and the operation count is O((V + E) log V) rather than the O(E + V log V) that decrease-key buys. Cite p. 597 for the decrease-key contract and p. 610 for the operation count that assumes it.
- **It settles the "algorithmic vs. data-structural" question in the report's favour, in the authors' own words.** They describe their contribution as "our implementation of Dijkstra's algorithm" (p. 598) and Section 4 reproduces Dijkstra's Scan step verbatim in modern notation. Combined with the Dijkstra (1959) notes — where no container is specified at all — the report can assert that the entire O(V²) → O(E + V log V) span is data-structure choice, with a primary citation at each end.
- **It is the right source for the non-negativity precondition** that Dijkstra (1959) never states: p. 596 ("with nonnegative edge lengths") and p. 610 ("The nonnegativity of the edge lengths implies that a vertex, once scanned, can never become labeled").
- **The optimality remark (p. 613) is a good closing note for the lessons-learned section:** with a good enough priority queue, Dijkstra's algorithm hits the comparison-model lower bound Ω(n log n + m). There is no further gain to be had by improving the container alone — which is a principled reason the report's technique has a ceiling, and a sourced one.
- **The four-pointers-per-node remark (p. 604) is quotable support for the constant-factor argument** the report needs to explain why the asymptotically superior structure loses at small V or high density. The authors themselves price their structure in pointers and note the constant-factor cost of shrinking it.

## Cautions

- **Do NOT cite this paper for the report's lazy-deletion variant.** The paper's "lazy deletion" (Sec. 3, pp. 605–606, credited to Cheriton and Tarjan) is a *different technique*: it leaves **vacant nodes** in a Fibonacci heap after a delete and sweeps them during a later find min. The report's variant pushes **duplicate (node, dist) entries** into a binary heap and discards stale pops. Both are called "lazy deletion" and both trade space for time, but they are not the same construction and this paper is not a source for the duplicate-entry technique. If the report needs a citation for the duplicate-entry variant, look elsewhere (it is folklore, typically credited to textbook/competitive-programming presentations); otherwise present it as an implementation choice and simply describe it.
- **Do NOT cite this paper for the O((V+E) log V) binary-heap bound.** The paper never states it. Its baseline for comparison is *Johnson's* `O(m log_(m/n+2) n)` d-ary-heap bound (ref. [13]), not the binary heap. For O((V+E) log V), cite Johnson (1977) or a standard textbook, not Fredman & Tarjan.
- **Do NOT claim the paper reports any measured or experimental runtimes.** It is purely analytic — worst-case and amortized bounds, no benchmarks, no constant factors, no machine. The only nod to practice is a reference to Brown's experimental study of *binomial* queues (p. 597), which is someone else's work on a different structure. Any claim about Fibonacci heaps' well-known poor practical constants is *not* from this source.
- **Do NOT present the report's binary heap as a Fibonacci heap or claim the O(E + V log V) bound for it.** The report implements a binary min-heap; O(E + V log V) requires the F-heap's O(1) amortized decrease-key, which the report's structure does not provide (and, using lazy deletion, deliberately does not attempt).
- **Do NOT report the bound as amortized *for the shortest-path algorithm*.** Theorem 1 (p. 604) gives amortized bounds *per heap operation*; the resulting O(n log n + m) for Dijkstra is a **worst-case** bound on the whole run, as the abstract says ("we obtain the following worst-case bounds"). Do not blur the two.
- **Do NOT date the JACM article 1984 or page it 338–346.** That is the FOCS preliminary version (DOI 10.1109/SFCS.1984.715934). Semantic Scholar's record merges them and gets this wrong; so did at least one search snippet, which also mis-stated the page range as 569–615. The JACM article is 1987, 34(3), 596–615.
- **Do NOT claim Fibonacci heaps are optimal for the shortest-path *problem*.** The paper's own open question (ii) on p. 613 says the Ω(n log n + m) lower bound applies to Dijkstra's *algorithm* in the comparison model and explicitly "does not preclude the existence of another, faster algorithm".
- **Do NOT use "Journal of the Association for Computing Machinery" and "Journal of the ACM" interchangeably within the report.** Both are defensible; pick one and be consistent. The APA entry above uses *Journal of the ACM*.
